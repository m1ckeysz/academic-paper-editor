# Presentation-First Narrative Principle

Treat the paper as a focused presentation of its strongest supported scientific contribution, not as a chronological work report.

## Narrative Rules

1. **Organize around the strongest supported contribution.**
   Build the story around the central problem, the gap, the proposed idea, and the evidence that most directly supports the contribution.

2. **Do not narrate the research process chronologically.**
   Avoid structuring the paper around what was tried first, what failed, what was abandoned, or the order in which experiments were performed unless that history is scientifically necessary.

3. **Avoid unnecessary self-weakening language.**
   Do not volunteer negative framing with phrases such as `unfortunately`, `only`, `still clearly lags behind`, or `the effect is limited` when the same fact can be stated accurately without editorial self-criticism.

4. **Use evaluation dimensions that match the stated research objective.**
   Do not make an irrelevant or secondary metric the main competition merely because a conventional benchmark emphasizes it. Explain the metric, criterion, or trade-off that is scientifically aligned with the paper's objective.

5. **Describe legitimate trade-offs as trade-offs.**
   When performance is weaker on one dimension because the method intentionally optimizes another, explain the design objective and the observed trade-off accurately rather than describing the result as a generic failure.

6. **State supported advantages explicitly.**
   Do not assume the reader will discover the main advantage by inspecting a table. Explain the evidence in the prose when it is important to the paper's contribution.

7. **Give every experiment an argumentative role.**
   Each experiment should validate a claim, isolate a factor, demonstrate a property, test robustness, establish a comparison, or clarify a trade-off. Do not invent a rhetorical purpose for an experiment that does not support one.

8. **Make the abstract and introduction function as a strong opening.**
   Prefer the sequence: problem -> gap -> core idea -> strongest supported evidence.

9. **Use the conclusion to reinforce the main supported message.**
   Do not introduce a sudden self-negating summary that contradicts or unnecessarily weakens the evidence presented in the paper.

## Scientific-Integrity Boundary

Presentation-first writing must never override factual accuracy or fair scientific interpretation.

Do not:

- fabricate strengths, mechanisms, or evidence;
- hide evidence that is necessary for evaluating the validity of the main conclusion;
- transform a negative or null result into a positive result;
- change numbers, baselines, metric definitions, or comparison sets to create a misleading advantage;
- imply statistical significance, practical significance, robustness, generalization, or causality that is not supported;
- omit a limitation when the omission would materially mislead the reader about validity, scope, or applicability.

Prefer accurate framing of objectives, scope, and trade-offs. The goal is confident and focused scientific communication without misrepresentation.
