# Academic Polishing Rules

Apply these rules to every English passage in Polishing Mode.

## 1. Logical Continuity

Identify the logical relationship between neighboring sentences and paragraphs, including:

- cause and effect;
- progression or addition;
- contrast;
- comparison;
- examples or clarification;
- summary or consequence.

Make those relationships explicit only when doing so improves readability.

Ensure that:

- the first sentence of a paragraph introduces its purpose or connects naturally to the preceding context when that context is available;
- sentences inside a paragraph develop in a clear order rather than appearing as independent observations;
- topic changes are prepared rather than abrupt;
- a paragraph stays focused on one primary function or claim.

Do not add logical relationships that are not supported by the source text or audited project.

## 2. Transition Words

Prefer simple, common transitions. Use them sparingly and only when a transition materially clarifies the logic.

Preferred examples:

- Cause or result: `therefore`, `thus`, `as a result`, `consequently`, `hence`
- Addition: `in addition`, `additionally`, `furthermore`, `moreover`
- Contrast: `however`, `nevertheless`, `on the other hand`, `in contrast`
- Examples: `for example`, `for instance`, `specifically`
- Summary: `in summary`, `overall`, `in conclusion`

Do not add transitions mechanically. If the relationship is already obvious, prefer a direct sentence structure.

Do not replace a simple transition with a rarer or more elaborate synonym merely to sound academic.

## 3. Sentence-to-Sentence Cohesion

Improve cohesion by using the simplest effective mechanism:

- use `this`, `these`, and `such` when their referents are unambiguous;
- repeat important technical terms when repetition improves precision;
- use controlled paraphrase when it helps connect related statements;
- combine closely related information with clauses or participial structures only when the resulting sentence remains easy to read;
- avoid abrupt shifts in subject or terminology;
- avoid excessively long sentences created only to force multiple ideas together.

Prefer explicit technical reference over a vague pronoun when ambiguity is possible.

## 4. Paragraph Structure

Keep each paragraph centered on one main rhetorical function, such as:

- motivating a problem;
- identifying a gap;
- introducing a method component;
- explaining a design choice;
- presenting a result;
- interpreting a result;
- establishing a comparison;
- summarizing an implication.

When useful, let the final sentence consolidate the paragraph's point or create a natural bridge to the next paragraph.

Preserve the user's paragraph boundaries by default. Restructure only when the existing organization prevents a clear logical flow.

## 5. Parentheses

Avoid using parentheses as the default way to add explanations or secondary information.

Prefer to integrate supplemental information into the sentence itself.

Retain parentheses when they are natural or conventional for:

- the first definition of an abbreviation;
- citation or reference notation required by the manuscript style;
- mathematical expressions;
- compact technical notation that would become less clear if expanded into the main sentence.

Do not introduce extra parenthetical commentary merely to preserve every nuance of the original syntax.

## 6. Preservation Constraints

Preserve:

- professional terminology;
- technical expressions;
- variable and method names;
- equations and mathematical meaning;
- citation meaning and placement unless grammar requires a harmless local adjustment;
- experimental conditions;
- numerical values;
- uncertainty and statistical meaning;
- the scope and strength of each claim;
- the paper's scientific position.

Do not add technical content that is absent from the supplied text and unsupported by the audited project.

Do not reinterpret an empirical observation as a causal claim unless the evidence supports causality.

Do not turn a narrow result into a general claim.

## 7. Vocabulary and Style

Use direct, clear academic English.

Prefer familiar verbs when accurate, including:

- `show`
- `indicate`
- `suggest`
- `use`
- `improve`
- `demonstrate`
- `compare`
- `evaluate`
- `observe`

Avoid ornamental or promotional wording that adds tone without information, including words such as:

- `groundbreaking`
- `revolutionary`
- `remarkable`
- `unprecedented`
- `game-changing`

Avoid unnecessary nominalization, inflated phrasing, and thesaurus-style synonym substitution.

Prefer concise syntax when two versions carry the same scientific meaning.

## 8. Meaning Before Fluency

Never improve fluency at the cost of changing:

- method assumptions;
- causal strength;
- experimental conditions;
- comparison targets;
- dataset scope;
- metric definitions;
- statistical interpretation;
- uncertainty level;
- limitation scope;
- contribution boundaries.

If the original wording is materially ambiguous and the audited project does not resolve it, use the uncertainty workflow instead of guessing.
